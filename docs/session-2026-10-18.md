## 1. Guest speaker prep: how physical motion gets made

The class's guest speaker is a AAA technical artist (procedural animation, motion-capture pipelines, Control Rig). Physical motion is a settled pillar of this game, so we drafted three questions for him and answered them from public sources first, so the questions can go deeper.

1. **Stumble-and-recover on a budget.** AAA uses *driven ragdolls*: the ragdoll follows the animation and physics takes over on contact (EA/Frostbite, GDC: "Physics Driven Ragdolls and Animation at EA"). Reactions are tiered: small hit = physical flinch layered over the animation; medium = stagger with a balance controller stepping to catch the body (capture point / inverted pendulum, the robotics model); big = full ragdoll, then blend into a get-up. The cheap version is what GDD §4.8 already plans: kinematic motion, partial physics on events, a get-up.
2. **Rigging a non-humanoid swimmer.** The closest match is argonautcode's "A simple procedural animation technique" (video + `animal-proc-anim` repo): a spine of distance-constrained points with bend limits, legs as FABRIK chains that step when they trail too far, and swimming as a traveling wave `cos(ωt − kx)` whose amplitude grows toward the tail. Gills are secondary motion (springs), not physics.
3. **Web vs. Unreal.** Rapier runs in the browser and three.js ships `RapierPhysics.js`; working three.js + Rapier ragdolls exist (`mattvb91/rapierjs-ragdoll`, a skinned-mesh one on r/threejs). Gaps: motors only on single-axis joints, no per-joint stiffness/damping on ball joints in the JS API (rapier.js issue #287, planned), and builders report stiff "rigor mortis" ragdolls from tight angle limits. Unreal ships Chaos + Physics Control; on the web we build it.

Questions for the speaker: which parts of this he'd disagree with, and how he'd rig and drive an axolotl.
